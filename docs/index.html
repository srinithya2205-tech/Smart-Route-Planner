PROJECT OVERVIEW

About SMART ROUTE

How the map, routing backend and graph-learning lab fit together.

Aim

Teach graph theory by letting students operate a network (logistics or biology), predict what an algorithm will do, then verify it step by step.

Learning outcomes
  1. Model a real system as a weighted graph.
  2. Choose an algorithm from the problem’s constraints.
  3. Trace its steps and state its complexity.
  4. Explain why it fails or succeeds (negative weights, disconnected graphs).
Who it is for

First- and second-year computing, mathematics and life-science students. No prior graph theory is needed.

Implementation

Each algorithm records its own steps, so playback shows real computation. Dijkstra and A* use a binary heap. Inputs are validated: negative weights block Dijkstra and A*, Bellman-Ford reports negative cycles, and unreachable targets and disconnected graphs are reported. Time, cost and fuel are derived from distance. The graph is saved in localStorage. The Graph Lab runs its teaching algorithms in the browser and records their steps for playback. The Route Planner calls a Python FastAPI backend, which validates route requests and proxies them to FOSSGIS-hosted OSRM. The backend returns route geometry, distance, estimated duration and navigation steps; the browser draws those results on Leaflet.

Assessment

A 12-question quiz (Easy, Medium, Hard) with hints, streak points, shuffled order and a review of mistakes. It covers shortest paths, spanning trees, traversal, complexity and the biology scenarios.

Limitations

Biology weights are illustrative, not measured. Prim uses a simple scan to keep steps visible. Multi-stop routing (TSP/VRP) is not implemented. The Route Planner needs internet access. Place search is global through Photon, and visible-map points of interest are loaded from OpenStreetMap; coverage depends on what contributors have mapped. The curated named pins in this build are concentrated around Bengaluru. The public search and routing services are shared best-effort services and are not guaranteed production infrastructure.

Verification

The self-test runs 300 random graphs per check against an independent Floyd–Warshall implementation, and compares Prim with Kruskal.

Map data quality & limits

Green Line station order and the 32-stop route have been updated. JAIN Global Campus is shown with separate main-gate and campus-footprint references; the interior point is not a road entrance. DSU is positioned using a GPS reference published by the university. Other curated citywide colleges, hospitals, restaurants, temples and parks are area-reference pins unless their popup says otherwise. The earlier auto-generated random business offsets were removed because they were not verified addresses. Local businesses and other mapped points of interest are loaded from OpenStreetMap around the current map view at zoom 16+, and road geometry can be overlaid from zoom 14+ when enabled. Places absent from OpenStreetMap or without a dependable public location cannot be pinned accurately.

Real-world applications
  • Navigation and delivery: road networks are weighted graphs, and routing engines use Dijkstra/A*-style searches (usually with speed-ups).
  • Computer networks: the OSPF routing protocol runs Dijkstra on the network graph.
  • Infrastructure design: minimum spanning trees (Prim, Kruskal) cut the cost of cable, pipe and road layouts.
  • Emergency services: shortest paths on one-way-aware road graphs for dispatch.
  • Epidemiology: BFS models spread by contact generations.
  • Biology: neural circuits and metabolic networks are analysed as directed graphs.
